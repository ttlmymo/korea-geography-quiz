About Jongno-gu

Located in a feng shui-blessed basin with Baekak Mountain as its central peak and Naksan and Inwangsan Mountains flanking it on either side, this is the heart of the capital that served as the seat of the Joseon Dynasty for 600 years. It is an autonomous district that encapsulates Seoul’s politics, history, and culture, home to the palaces of Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, and Gyeonghuigung; the Jongmyo Shrine and Sajikdan Altar; the Blue House and government agencies; and the Jongno commercial district extending from Unjongga.

History of Jongno-gu

In ancient times, it served as the stage for the Jin and Mahan confederacies and early Baekje. After the rivalry among the Three Kingdoms, it became Hanyang County during the Unified Silla period and was part of Yangju and Namgyeong during the Goryeo period—a strategic location so important that it was considered a candidate for the royal capital. In 1394, when King Taejo Yi Seong-gye moved the capital to Hanyang, the palace, the Jongmyo Shrine, the Sajik Altar, the city walls, government offices, market streets, and roads were established in this area according to the principles of ‘palaces on the left, government offices on the right’ and ‘ancestral shrines in front, markets behind,’ making it the center of the royal capital. Unjongga, lined with the Six Market Halls, formed the core of the city center. In 1943, with the implementation of the district system, Jongno-gu was established, taking its name from the bell tower (Jongnu) in Unjongga. In 1975, it incorporated parts of Seodaemun-gu and Dongdaemun-gu to reach its current boundaries. Major events in modern and contemporary history, including the March 1st Movement and the April 19 Revolution, took place here, establishing the area as a symbolic stage for politics and the democracy movement.

People & Stories

Jongno-gu is home to a concentration of sites symbolic of the Joseon Dynasty—including the Jongmyo Shrine and Changdeokgung Palace, both designated as UNESCO World Heritage Sites, as well as national treasure-level palace architecture such as Geunjeongjeon Hall and Gyeonghoeru Pavilion at Gyeongbokgung Palace, along with the Sajikdan Altar, Munmyo Confucian Shrine, and Sungkyunkwan University—accounting for about half of Seoul’s designated cultural heritage sites. On Bibong Peak of Mount Samgak, there is the site of King Jinheung’s inspection tour, which illustrates Silla’s territorial expansion, while the Ten-Story Stone Pagoda at Wongaksa Temple Site and Tapgol Park together bear witness to the history of Joseon Buddhism and the March 1st Movement. In addition, numerous folktales related to the capital—such as the legend of Seonbawi Rock, which recounts the dispute between Jeong Do-jeon and Muhak over the location of the capital, and the legend of Chimabawi Rock on Inwangsan Mountain—as well as folk songs like the 'Gyeongbokgung Taryeong' and the 'Hangangsu Taryeong' have been passed down through the generations.
