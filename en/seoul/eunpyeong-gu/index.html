About Eunpyeong-gu

This autonomous district is located in a basin at the northwestern tip of Seoul, surrounded by the foothills of Mount Samgak (Bukhansan) and Mount Baekryeon. As a key stop along the old official road connecting Seoul to Uiju, it is home to many historic temples such as Jingwansa and Samcheonsa, and serves as a leading residential area in the northwest, featuring Eunpyeong New Town and the Yeonsinnae commercial district.

History of Eunpyeong-gu

In ancient times, it was part of the Mahan Confederacy and early Baekje. After the power struggles among the Three Kingdoms, it became part of Hanyang County during the Unified Silla period and belonged to Yangju during the Goryeo Dynasty. It was a strategic location at the foot of Mount Samgak, home to Yeongseo Station and temples such as Jingwansa and Samcheonsa. During the Joseon Dynasty, it belonged to the Yeon-eun-bang and Sangpyeong-bang districts in the northern part of Hanseong-bu; the name ‘Eunpyeong’ itself derives from the combination of these two place names. As the gateway to Uiju, through which Chinese envoys traveled, it was a strategically important crossroads in terms of politics, diplomacy, and military affairs. In 1914, it became Eunpyeong-myeon in Goyang County, but in 1949, it was incorporated into Seoul and became part of Seodaemun-gu. In 1979, it was separated from Seodaemun-gu to form the newly established Eunpyeong-gu. Since the 1960s, land readjustment in the Bulgwang and Yeokchon districts has transformed farmland into residential lots, and the area has recently been transformed into a new residential district through the Eunpyeong New Town development.

People & Stories

On Bibong Peak of Mount Samgaksan in Jingwan-oe-dong, there is the site of King Jinheung’s royal inspection stele on Mount Bukhansan, which attests to the expansion of the Silla realm; the original stele is preserved at the National Museum of Korea. In the Imalsan area of Jingwannae-dong, there is a burial ground for eunuchs of the Joseon royal court, which serves as a valuable historical record of their lives. Additionally, various folk tales have been passed down, such as the story of 'Grandma Bap,' who is said to have disguised Nojeokbong Peak as a rice sack and poured lime into the Changneung Stream to repel Japanese invaders during the Imjin War, and the legend of Won Du-pyo of Dokbakgol, which is linked to the plot for the Injo Restoration.
