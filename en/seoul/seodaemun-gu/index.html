About Seodaemun-gu

Located in northwestern Seoul, just outside Donuimun (Seodaemun)—one of the four major gates of the old capital—this autonomous district blends the green spaces at the foothills of Inwangsan and Ansan mountains with the university district of Sinchon. It is an educational hub with a high concentration of universities, including Yonsei University and Ewha Womans University, and a symbolic site of the anti-Japanese independence movement, home to Independence Gate and the Seodaemun Prison History Museum.

History of Seodaemun-gu

During the Three Kingdoms period, it was a border region where Baekje, Goguryeo, and Silla vied for control of the Han River basin. During the Goryeo Dynasty, it was known as Namgyeong, and after the founding of the Joseon Dynasty, it was incorporated into Hanseong-bu, serving as the western gateway to the capital. The area was home to Mohwagwan and Yeongeunmun, where envoys from the Ming and Qing dynasties were received, serving as a gateway for foreign exchanges; during the late Joseon period, it belonged to the Banseok-bang and Bansong-bang districts, as well as the Yeonhui and Yeon-eun-bang areas of Seongjeo-sipri. In 1943, Seodaemun-gu was established following the implementation of the district system; subsequently, Mapo-gu was separated in 1944 and Eunpyeong-gu in 1979, resulting in the district’s current configuration. In modern times, with the establishment of universities and missionary organizations in the Sinchon area, it has grown into a leading educational and cultural hub representing Seoul.

People & Stories

In Hyeonjeo-dong, Independence Gate—erected by the Independence Association on the site of Yeongeunmun Gate, where Qing envoys were once received—still stands as a symbol of the will for independence. Next to it, Seodaemun Prison, where numerous independence activists endured imprisonment, has been preserved as a historical museum. Bongwon Temple in Bongwon-dong is said to have been founded by National Preceptor Doseon during the Silla period; it is a place where the Buddhist ritual Yeongsanjae is passed down as a National Intangible Cultural Heritage. Additionally, there is a legend passed down about a filial son who, while traveling back and forth to his father’s grave at Muakjae, was carried on the back of a tiger.
