Dongdaemun-gu, Seoul

동대문구 · 10 neighborhoods · Learn Korean administrative divisions with maps

About Dongdaemun-gu

Located in northeastern Seoul, just outside Heunginmun (Dongdaemun), this autonomous district has long served as the eastern gateway to the capital. It is a key transportation hub centered around Cheongnyangni Station and a center for the trade of traditional Korean medicine, home to Seoul Yakryeongsi (Gyeongdong Market). It is also a region steeped in history, where the Seonongdan—a site where kings of the Joseon Dynasty encouraged farming and performed ancestral rites—still stands.

History of Dongdaemun-gu

During the Three Kingdoms period, it was a border region where Baekje, Goguryeo, and Silla vied for control of the Han River basin. In the Joseon Dynasty, it was part of the ‘Seongjeo Sipri’ (Ten Li Outside the Capital) and was organized into the Sungshin-bang and Inchang-bang districts outside Heunginmun Gate. This area was symbolically central to the nation’s agricultural policy, as it was home to Seonnongdan—where the king performed the Seonnong ritual to pray for a bountiful harvest—and Dongjeokjeon (East Ritual Field), the royal farm. During the Japanese colonial period, the area grew into a transportation and commercial hub in eastern Seoul following the opening of Cheongnyangni Station. After liberation, administrative boundaries were adjusted several times, and in 1988, parts of the area—including Sinseol-dong, Yongdu-dong, and Jegi-dong—were separated to form Jungnang-gu. As a result, the district now consists of 10 neighborhoods: Jeonnong, Dapsimni, Jang-an, Hoe-gi, Cheongnyangni, Hwigeong, and Imun-dong.

People & Stories

At Seonnongdan in Jegi-dong, a juniper tree—said to have been planted in the early Joseon Dynasty—remains designated as a Natural Monument. It is also said that the rice soup the king shared with his people after performing the Seonnong ritual is the origin of today’s ‘seolleongtang.’ In Cheongnyangni-dong, Yeonghwiwon—the tomb of Consort Sunheon, Empress Dowager Eom, a concubine of King Gojong—and Sunginwon, the tomb of his young grandson Lee Jin, stand side by side, offering a glimpse into the burial rites of the Joseon royal family. Furthermore, in Dapsimni-dong and Jeonnong-dong, the tradition of village shrines (dongje-dang) where prayers are offered for the community’s well-being continues to this day.
