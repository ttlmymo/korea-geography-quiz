About Yeongdeungpo-gu

This autonomous district is located in southwestern Seoul, on a low, flat alluvial plain where the Han River and Anyang Stream converge. As the first area south of the Han River to be developed into an urban center, it serves as a hub for industry and commerce. Yeouido, an island in the middle of the river, is home to a high concentration of the National Assembly, broadcasting stations, and financial institutions, making it a key center for politics and the economy.

History of Yeongdeungpo-gu

In ancient times, it was part of the Mahan confederacy and early Baekje. After a period of rivalry among the Three Kingdoms, it belonged to Gogyang County during the Unified Silla period and to Geumju (衿州) during the Goryeo period. During the Joseon Dynasty, it was a rural area along the Han River corresponding to the Sangbuk and Habuk townships of Geumcheon and Siheung counties. During the Japanese colonial period, it grew into a major industrial area as factories—including spinning and flour mills—sprang up in large numbers, taking advantage of its location as the junction of the Gyeongin and Gyeongbu lines. In 1943, it became Yeongdeungpo-gu following the implementation of the district system. After liberation, it covered a vast area south of the Han River, but its boundaries were gradually reduced to their current size as Gwanak, Gangseo, and Guro districts were successively separated. Since the 1970s, with the development of Yeouido and the construction of bridges, it has established itself as a new center of Seoul and a hub for media and finance.

People & Stories

In Yeongdeungpo-dong 4-ga, the former Gyeongseong Textile Office Building—a symbol of the textile industry built with Korean capital during the Japanese colonial period—remains as a Nationally Registered Heritage Site, showcasing the history of modern industry and architecture. At Bamgoji Pass in Singil-dong, a tradition of holding the Bugundang ritual is passed down alongside a legend that when a funeral procession passed by, horses’ hooves would stick to the ground; there is also the folktale of Yun Jeong-seung at Banghakgot, where a carp is said to have saved his life. Furthermore, various Bugundang shrines—such as those in Dangsan-dong and Yeongdeungpo-dong—have been preserved, maintaining the folk belief traditions of riverside villages.
